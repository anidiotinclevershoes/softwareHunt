## Deep attack — SFX library packaging

**Observed job:** independent field recordists must consistently name, tag and embed metadata in large WAV batches before sale. A Sound Effect reports 600+ independent creators and 7,000+ libraries; Sound Effects Search lists hundreds of shops.

**Exact competition:** AudioCrucible proposes UCS names/keywords, writes BWF/iXML locally and plans a $179 one-time price with $59/year updates. Soundminer/BaseHead occupy professional library management. Sona’s exact-query-aligned generative pack page lacked independent commercial corroboration and was excluded as demand evidence.

**AI test:** audio classifiers can propose categories/descriptions and make the product cheap to build. Reliability, local processing and metadata-writing justify dedicated software, but AudioCrucible already makes precisely that case.

**Second kill:** even perfect execution faces roughly hundreds, not tens of thousands, of visible independent pack creators. At one-time pricing, acquisition and support would dominate.

**Verdict:** KILLED, not narrowed to an obscure file variant.

## Deep attack — production-music delivery packs

**Observed job:** production libraries require masters, stems, alternate mixes, exact formats, naming and metadata. Dramedy Box says missed steps cause resubmission and documents 24-bit/48kHz files, lead-in limits, stems and alternates.

**Exact competition:** Soniteq Kora/Export Flow claims naming enforcement, metadata checklists, version validation and batch package generation for $7–$25/month. Its independent traction was not corroborated, but the current live product and price are decisive direct competitive evidence. Other stem/export utilities also surfaced.

**AI tests:** AI can propose metadata and flag missing versions; DAW scripting can automate exports. Neither creates differentiated value once a music-native product already combines them.

**Customer access:** PCAM and Music Producers Guild expose many named composers and production companies.

**Second kill:** the obvious product is already available below the price needed for a small-customer high-income business. Switching must also touch DAW/project conventions.

**Verdict:** KILLED.

## Deep attack — dance competition edits

**Observed job:** dance schools pay to cut songs, combine tracks, remove explicit lyrics, create proper endings and meet routine lengths.

**Price evidence:** Cut My Music advertises $10 per completed edit; another specialist quotes $34.90. UK DanceMix quotes custom projects. GrooveCut advertises beat-locked cuts, count-ins and AI editing free during beta, but independent usage was not corroborated.

**AI tests:** general audio tools already cut, isolate and time-stretch; a dedicated product can improve beat alignment and dance vocabulary. That remaining benefit is exactly what GrooveCut advertises.

**Customer access:** BDSA, NATD and British Dance Council directories expose large numbers of UK dance schools.

**Second kill:** at $10–$35 per output, €5k monthly needs roughly 140–500 paid edits before support, payment and acquisition costs. Free tools and specialists are already present.

**Verdict:** KILLED.

## Deep attack — choir rehearsal tracks

**Observed job:** a conductor supplies a score and receives full mix, accompaniment and part-dominant/muted practice tracks. Public services charge $180 and £250 per score.

**Competitors:** Chorilo automatically generates practice audio for every MusicXML part and adds moving score, adjustable tempo/key and pitch feedback. SightSinger and downloadable track catalogues cover adjacent routes; human custom-track services cover messy inputs.

**Why it initially survived:** the finished result is complete, the price is material, a choir repeats it across repertoire, and Making Music exposes thousands of groups, including a public list with far more than ten named choirs.

**AI test A:** clean MusicXML can already produce most of the output deterministically. PDF/image scores require optical music recognition and correction; synthetic sung text and expressive phrasing remain imperfect.

**AI test B:** AI makes recognition and vocal rendering progressively cheaper, but the product must still verify notation, lyrics, repeats, divisi and rights. Errors directly harm rehearsal.

**Separate second kill:** a solo non-musician founder inherits transcription exceptions, musical support and copyright questions. Removing those cases leaves only clean MusicXML, which Chorilo already handles.

**Verdict:** **ANOMALY ONLY.** Do not outreach or build. New evidence trigger: repeated requests from choirs that already have licensed digital scores but reject current automatic sound/UX and currently pay £180–£250.

## Deep attack — font release QA

**Observed job:** small foundries need contour, naming, metrics, hinting, glyph-coverage and cross-application QA before release. Professional mastering studios sell the human service.

**Exact/free alternative:** FontBakery is open source, supports OpenType/UFO/Glyphs inputs and distributor profiles, and is designed for continuous QA. Fontspector is adjacent.

**AI tests:** AI can explain failures or suggest fixes, but that is not the trusted acceptance layer. A dedicated UI is cheap to build but wraps a free engine.

**Customer access:** public foundry directories expose hundreds of named independent foundries.

**Second kill:** the separable deterministic layer is free; the paid remainder requires expert font engineering and application testing. A friendlier interface is the prohibited “better feature” proposition.

**Verdict:** KILLED.
